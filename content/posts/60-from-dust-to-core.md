*Year 12,026 of the Common Reckoning. The Terran Galactic Empire.*

---

## I. Karsk-9

On Karsk-9, water was counted the way other worlds counted money.

Mira Vael knew exactly how many cups her family drank each day, because she was the one who wrote it on the kitchen wall in chalk. Her parents were harvest-farmers for the Empire, which meant they coaxed a hardy, grey-green grain called *sorrel-wheat* out of rust-colored soil, loaded it onto freighters, and never once tasted it. The grain fed fleets. The fleets fed the Empire. Nobody on the fleets knew Karsk-9's name.

In sixteen years, Mira had met perhaps fifteen people. Her parents. The neighbors two farms over. The freighter crew who came twice a year and never took off their helmets.

She fixed the irrigation drones when they failed, and then she improved them. She rewrote their routing code until the farm used a fifth less water. She did it by lamplight, after chores, because the numbers were the only thing in her life that felt infinite.

Then came Assessment Day.

Every year, an Imperial survey ship passed over the outer worlds, and every sixteen-year-old sat before an automated terminal for three hours. Most were told, politely, to return to the fields. Mira finished early, and the terminal went quiet for a long time.

Then it glowed gold.

> **CANDIDATE VAEL, MIRA. PERCENTILE: 99.998. YOU ARE HEREBY SUMMONED TO THE IMPERIAL ACADEMY, CAPITAL WORLD OF AURELION.**

Her mother cried. Her father said nothing.

It was the officer from the survey ship who told them the rest, gently, as if reading from a card he had read many times. Passage to Aurelion was paid by the Academy. Passage *back* was not. A return fare cost more than the farm earned in thirty years. Scholars did not come home. They were assigned posts, and the posts were wherever the Empire needed them.

Mira watched her parents do the same arithmetic she had just done. Her father was fifty-one. Her mother, forty-eight. Karsk-9 had one ship a year, if that.

"So this is goodbye," her mother said. She did not say it as a question.

---

The last night, nobody slept. Her mother baked the good bread, the one she saved for harvest, using a full week's flour. Her father sat by the lamp mending Mira's boots, though they were already mended, resoling them with leather he had been saving for something else. He would not look up.

At dawn, her mother pressed a small tin cup into her hands. It was the cup Mira had drunk from every day of her life. Its rim was dented where she had dropped it as a toddler.

"So you remember how much is enough," her mother said.

Then she took the chalk, walked to the kitchen wall, and beneath the column of Mira's careful numbers she wrote, in her slow, unpracticed hand:

*Mira. Home.*

At the landing field, the shuttle's engines screamed and the red dust rose in sheets. Mira turned back at the top of the ramp. Her mother was waving with both arms, like a child. Her father stood absolutely still, the way he stood in the fields when the dust-storms came, as if he could hold the whole sky up by refusing to move.

Mira watched them through the window until the ship climbed above the clouds and they were smaller than the numbers on the wall. Then smaller than grains of wheat. Then gone.

She knew she would never see their faces again.

---

## II. Aurelion

The journey took eleven days. Mira spent them in silence, holding the tin cup in both hands.

The shuttle docked at a terminal the size of a mountain. She followed the line of passengers down a corridor, through a gate, past a scanner that chimed at her and let her through, and then she stepped out onto the open concourse.

And the world exploded.

It hit her first as **noise**. A roar with no beginning and no end, like standing inside a storm made of voices. Then it hit her as **light**: walls of moving color, advertisements the height of buildings, shifting and chattering in languages she half-recognized.

Then she looked up, and she did not stop looking up.

There was no sky. Where the sky should have been, there were *layers*. A layer of towers, then a layer of bridges, then a layer of glass walkways crowded with tiny figures, and above that another layer, and another, and another, climbing so high the highest ones disappeared into haze. She tried to count them and lost track at forty. Between the levels, flying cars streamed in lanes like rivers of light, thousands upon thousands of them, humming past in silent, glittering rows. Below her feet, through a transparent floor, she could see *more city*, falling away into depths that had no bottom.

And the people.

Mira had met fifteen people in her entire life. In the first ten seconds, she passed more than a thousand. They flowed around her in a living current, shoulder to shoulder, talking, shouting, laughing, not one of them looking at her. A man in a coat of shifting silver collided with her case and said something sharp without breaking stride. A group of girls her age swept past in a cloud of perfume and sparks. Someone's pet, a thing with too many legs, trotted by on a glowing leash.

*Millions*, her mind whispered. *A whole planet of this. A planet that is one single city.*

Her heart pounded so hard it hurt. The air was wet and thick, heavy with smells she could not name: hot metal, sugar, rain, smoke, flowers, something burning, something sweet. Beside her, water ran in a clear gutter along the edge of the walkway, an inch deep, rushing away down a drain. Nobody glanced at it. A girl poured half a bottle into it, shook the last drops out, and kept walking.

Mira wanted to reach down and scoop it up, all of it, before it was gone.

She stood in the middle of the concourse, a small figure in mended boots, clutching a dented tin cup, while the whole Empire poured around her like she was a stone in a river.

She was a fish out of its bowl. And the air here had no water in it that she knew how to breathe.

---

## III. The First Night

Mira was a genius. The terminal on Karsk-9 had said so, with a percentile.

It turned out the percentile did not cover this.

Her slate told her to report to *Dormitory Hall 7-Gamma, Tier 212*. It did not tell her how. She stood at a transit junction with thirty glowing platforms, each marked with shifting symbols she couldn't parse, and watched people step onto them and vanish sideways, upward, diagonally. She tried one. It carried her *down* four hundred floors before she could find a way off. She tried another. It dropped her in a market where a vendor shouted at her in a dialect so fast she understood none of it.

Her slate chimed. **CIVIC CHIP NOT DETECTED. ACCESS DENIED.**

She had not known she needed one. Every door in Aurelion seemed to need one. The lifts refused her. The public restrooms refused her. The little machine selling food refused her, and a guard looked at her boots, then at her face, and told her curtly to move along.

By the time the artificial lights dimmed to a dusk-color, she had been walking for nine hours. She was lost, and the slate's battery was at four percent. She had not eaten since the ship, and she was too afraid to ask anyone for anything, because every face looked so certain, so busy, so completely uninterested.

She found a stairwell. Concrete, dim, a faint hum in the walls. She sat on the third step with her case against the wall and tried to *think*.

She was good at problems. Break them down. Find the variables. But there were no variables here, only a million things she didn't know, and she didn't even know which ones mattered. She could not calculate her way out of not knowing *how a door worked*.

The city did not go dark. It did not even go quiet. Somewhere below, a train howled by every few minutes. Somewhere above, someone was laughing. Light seeped in through a vent, the color of nothing, and she realized that she had not seen a star since she left home.

She tried counting. Steps. Breaths. The hum of the wall. The numbers kept slipping away.

Then, out of nowhere, her stomach cramped with thirst.

At the bottom of the stairwell, she found a public tap. A plain metal spout, no lock, no chip, no fee. She stared at it for a long time. She crept down, turned the handle, and the water came out clear and cold and *endless*.

Her hands were shaking. She filled the tin cup.

She drank half and stopped, out of habit, to save the rest. Then she stopped herself, because there was no one to save it for. She emptied the cup and filled it again. And again. She drank until her stomach ached, until water ran down her chin and soaked the front of her shirt, and the whole time, her face crumpled, and the sound that came out of her was not a word.

She thought of the chalk on the kitchen wall. *Mira. Home.*

Then she climbed back to the third step, curled around the cup, and tried to sleep, with the whole roaring city turning above her like an engine that would never stop.

Tomorrow, she would find the dormitory. Tomorrow, she would figure out the doors.

But tonight, for the first time in her life, Mira Vael had no idea what to do. And nobody, anywhere, was coming to tell her.

*The End*
