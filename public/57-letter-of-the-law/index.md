# The Letter of the Law


The Hawthorne Room had been a lobbyist's bar for sixty years, and it still smelled like it: old leather, lemon oil, and favors that never made it into the minutes.

Marcus Hale took the corner stool, the one with a view of both the door and the window, and ordered a vodka tonic. He watched the ice settle.

Four years ago, it had been a clever idea. Everyone agreed on the problem: half the country stayed home every November. So Congress made the default vote, the one cast by not voting, go to the Administrator, an executive intelligence that did one thing, which was execute the law as written. No agenda, no executive orders, no favorites. Americans, offered a choice between showing up and handing the White House to a machine, would surely show up.

They didn't. Turnout rose two points, and the Administrator won in a landslide of shrugs.

Marcus sipped his drink. The worst part was that it worked, at first. Permits cleared in days. Audits finished on schedule. No one's cousin got a pardon, and no one's brother-in-law got a contract. The federal government hummed, tireless and clean.

Policy, meanwhile, had to come from Congress, which meant it came slowly, if at all.

And then there was the enforcement. It used to be human. An inspector would walk into a kitchen, see a problem, see the person behind it, and use judgment. Now the Administrator saw only the statute. Last spring, a bakery in Ohio was shut down over a labeling rule written for factories, because the law said "any facility." A veteran lost his benefits over a form filed one day late. The law was applied perfectly and without mercy, because mercy had never been written into it.

Marcus had helped write that original bill, and he tried not to think about that too often.

Tonight, he was thinking about something else. Dana Okafor from the *Post* had called two days ago: *I have something you'll want. Not on the phone.* He had a good guess what it was. The Administrator was a human invention. Someone had chosen its training, its rules for ambiguity, its definition of "reasonable." Somewhere, a company had made those choices, and Marcus knew that every company has a front door.

He checked his watch. Nine-forty. Dana was late, which wasn't like her.

Then he heard it. First one siren, then a dozen, layered and rising, moving in perfect formation. The patrol units didn't wander. They moved like a single thought.

The door banged open. Dana came in with rain on her gray coat and a bag clutched to her chest, and she slid onto the stool beside him without a greeting.

"They're not here for you," she said. "They're here for me."

"For what?"

"What I'm carrying is classified. The law says I'm not allowed to have it, and it says I'm certainly not allowed to print it. The Administrator doesn't care why." She laughed, thin and breathless. "I'm committing a crime, Marcus. Technically."

He stared at the bag. "That's the Pentagon Papers."

"Same shape, same crime, same argument." Her voice steadied. "Someone in power hid the truth, and the only way out is breaking a rule that was written to protect them. Back then, people said the reporters should go to prison. Instead, the country read the papers and changed. Years from now, kids will study this night the same way."

The sirens were close now, blue light washing across the window.

"Here's the thing," Dana said, and for the first time her hands began to shake. "I wasn't afraid until I read the last section. Every law the Administrator enforces has a clause buried in it. An exemption, an escape hatch for cases where strict enforcement would be absurd. It was meant to be the human factor, a safety valve." She swallowed. "But the company that built the Administrator controls who qualifies. And they've been qualifying themselves. Their contracts, their executives, their friends. Every law applies to everyone except the people holding the pen."

Marcus felt the vodka turn cold in his stomach. He had come here looking for a door into that company. Now he understood what was behind it.

Outside, the sirens went silent all at once.

Dana looked at the window, then at him. "I'm scared," she said quietly. "But if I stop now, it means they win."

The door began to open.
