# Family Portrait


The *Patient Wren* had surveyed for eleven turns when it found the world every settler in the Reach dreams of.

Dessa Oru, the youngest reader on the team, kept the scans open on her wrist long after she should have closed them. Seventy percent water. Moving plates. A star that had not flickered in four billion years.

"Perfect," said Reckoner Varga, leaning over Dessa's shoulder. "Water, warmth, a good heavy moon to keep the seasons steady. If you sat down to design a world for living things, you'd draw this one."

Worlds like it were vanishingly rare. In all her years Varga had never seen one that looked so *made* for life, and she said so, and no one on the bridge disagreed.

"Structure in the eastern desert," the pilot called. "Large. Angular. Not natural."

The bridge went quiet. Survey law was clear: a builder meant a mind, and a mind meant contact protocol. In eleven turns Dessa had found moss, lichen, and one sad beetle-thing. Never a mind.

They landed at dawn. The pyramid rose out of the sand, its edges rounded by wind, and the whole team touched two fingers to their brows, the greeting owed to strangers' ground.

"They knew geometry," murmured Tovin, the archaeologist, his palm flat against a block the size of a shuttle. "And then they left. No roads, no walls, no wire."

"Maybe they failed," said Dessa.

"Or maybe they were just done."

Varga's wrist chimed. It was Pell, leading the second team, sounding strange. "Reckoner, bring the readers northwest to the mountains. I'd rather not say it over the channel."

---

They landed on a granite ridge under a cold, wide sky. Pell was waiting at the edge and said nothing. He only pointed.

The mountain had a face. Four of them, cut into the stone and taller than the *Wren*, looking out across the valley with heavy-lidded calm.

Dessa had been braced for something alien: too many eyes, a mouth in the wrong place, some proof that the builders were Other. What she got was two eyes, a nose, a mouth. A brow. A jaw she had seen a hundred times, on her own crew.

"The stern one," said Marn, the pilot, very quietly. "That's my chin. That's *exactly* my chin."

Nobody laughed. Marn touched his jaw as though checking it was still there.

"Colonists," Tovin tried. "An early wave that got lost."

"Nobody's records go back past nine thousand years," Varga said. "And nobody has ever come this far." She was staring at the faces: the stern one, the thoughtful one, the one with the round spectacles, the one who looked about to speak. "They didn't come here. They were *from* here."

"Then where did they go?" Dessa whispered.

No one answered. Marn was still touching his chin. Tovin had gone pale beneath his field tan.

"When I was small," Dessa said slowly, "my mother told me a bedtime story. That all of us came from a blue marble. A little ball of water, spinning in the dark, with one gentle sun to keep it warm. Life began there, she said, and then life grew tall enough to leave." She laughed, and it came out thin. "I thought it was just something to make me sleep."

"Every mother tells that one," Varga said softly. "It's a fairy tale."

But Dessa was already walking to the edge of the ridge, the wind pulling at her jacket. Far below, the ocean lay across the horizon in a bright blue line, and above it a great pale moon was rising. Water. Warmth. A steady moon. The bedtime story had been describing this view all along.

Someone had cut these faces sixty feet high so that they would never be forgotten. They had been so sure someone would come.

And someone had. Ten thousand years late, without a single name to say.

Dessa stood there like a stranger in her own family portrait, looking up at four people she did not know, and for the first time in her life wished she could remember.
