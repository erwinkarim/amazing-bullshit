# We Have A Situation


Franklin Delano Roosevelt rubbed his eyes, leaning back against the leather of his chair. It was late afternoon in 1933, and the Oval Office was stifling, thick with the scent of wool suits, damp rain, and fresh ink. Between navigating bank panics and drafting initial New Deal legislation, it had been a brutal, exhausting day.

The heavy oak door swung open, but it wasn't his trusted secretary, Louis Howe. A woman stepped inside wearing a sharp, dark pantsuit, tapping frantically on a glowing rectangle of glass. **"Mr. President, we have a situation,"** she said, her voice laced with urgent authority. **"The Chief of Staff needs you in the Situation Room immediately. Things are escalating quickly in Germany."**

Roosevelt frowned. *The Situation Room?* He had never heard of such a place. Before he could ask, she took the handles of his wheelchair and pushed him swiftly out into the corridor.

What lay beyond the threshold was not his White House. The familiar warm woodwork and soft plaster had vanished, replaced by sterile, smooth corridors bathed in cool, recessed light. Men and women darted past in casual attire with tiny coiled wires tucked into their ears, talking animatedly to empty air. Glowing slates hung on walls, displaying scrolling numbers and live video feeds that shifted at the touch of a finger. The air smelled faintly of ozone and artificial chill. Roosevelt gripped his armrests, his heart hammering against his ribs as he stared at the bewildering, impossible future unfolding around him.

She wheeled him through a sliding glass door into a darkened, windowless command center. **This was the Situation Room.** Dozens of aides sat at curved desks, their faces illuminated by the blue glare of monitors. High on the central wall hung a massive display showing a live, moving image of Berlin from directly overhead—a view from the heavens itself. Technicians shouted strange jargon about *satellite telemetry*, *encrypted signals*, and *thermal imaging*. Roosevelt stared, completely overwhelmed by the blinding speed and unimaginable technology, his mind reeling as he watched a distant continent tracked in real-time detail.

He opened his mouth to ask what year it was, but the air suddenly crackled with static electricity. A blinding flash of white light consumed the room, accompanied by a deafening, metallic hum.

When Roosevelt blinked away the glare, the noise was gone, replaced by the gentle ticking of a grandfather clock. He was back at his mahogany desk in 1933, surrounded by quiet lamplight and soft shadows.

Breathing heavily, he looked down at his desk blotter. Resting beside his fountain pen was a leaf of paper made of a smooth, unnaturally crisp material. Printed in flawless, perfectly uniform black lettering was a brief message:

*“Mr. President — Please accept our sincere apologies for the temporal displacement during system calibration. You were briefly integrated into White House Operations, Year 2026. The anomaly has been resolved. Please disregard.”*
