Sublevel Four

Dr. Elias Verhoeven had not seen the sun in eleven days, and he had stopped noticing. Down on Sublevel Four of the Halcyon Institute, the only light that mattered was the pale blue glow of the incubation chamber and the thin green line of a heartbeat on the monitor.

Eight years. Two hundred and six trials. He kept every name in his head. Marcus, whose heart simply stopped. Ines, whose muscles tore themselves from the bone. Some volunteers had come for the money. Others had come for the promise: a human being with no ceiling. Strength, speed, intellect, perception, every one at its maximum, all at once.

Every failure had taught him something. Every lesson had cost someone everything.

The two hundred and seventh

One vial remained on the steel tray, amber and faintly luminous. On the table lay Daniel Reyes, thirty-four, a former paramedic who had read every risk and signed anyway. “Somebody has to be the one it works on,” he had said.

“Vitals are stable,” said Dr. Amara Okoye, her voice barely above the hum of the machines. “Elias. Are we doing this?”

He loaded the injector with hands that had stopped shaking years ago. “Phase one. The carrier.”

The serum slid into the vein. For thirty seconds, nothing. Then Daniel’s back arched off the table. His heart rate leapt from forty to one-eighty to two-forty. His temperature climbed past forty-one degrees. Beneath the skin, muscle began to move, shifting and knitting and reorganizing itself like cable being rewound. On the scanner, his bone density climbed off the chart.

Amara’s hand shot toward the abort switch. Elias caught her wrist. “Wait.”

“He’s going to die like the others!”

“Wait.”

The alarms howled. Then, all at once, they stopped. The tremors faded. The heart rate fell and settled at thirty-two beats per minute, slow and deep and steady as a drum.

Daniel opened his eyes.

Peak

The restraints snapped like thread. He sat up, and the whole room seemed to lean toward him.

Within the hour he had solved a scrambled puzzle cube blindfolded in eleven seconds. He had read the two-hundred-page protocol once and recited it backwards. He caught a falling beaker without looking at it. He heard Amara’s pulse from across the room and told her, gently, that she was afraid. He noticed the flicker in the fluorescent tubes, a rhythm no one else in the room could see.

“Perfect,” Amara whispered.

Elias said nothing. He was crying. Eight years, two hundred and six graves, and at last a human being standing at the summit of what a human could be.

Daylight

The trouble began the morning they let him walk outside. Nobody had thought to test the sun. Daniel stepped through the loading-dock door at dawn, and the light hit his skin like acid. He went down screaming, his arms blistering in seconds, and Elias dragged him back into the dark. The scans showed eyes so sensitive that an ordinary sunrise was like staring into a welding arc.

Then came the food. Cooked meals came back up. He grew thin even as he ate, his new muscle starving on a diet that had fed him all his life. Raw meat worked better. Rarer meat worked better still. Finally Amara ran the numbers and set down her pen. His body had been optimized so completely that it tolerated no waste. Cooking broke the proteins down; digestion lost too much on the way. Blood was different. Iron, plasma, oxygen carriers, everything already assembled and ready to use.

The perfect human was the most efficient predator ever made.

“Every gift has a price,” Elias wrote in the log that night. “We never thought to read the fine print.”

What they called him

Daniel never saw daylight again. He learned the night instead, where he was faster than anything alive and could see clearly by starlight. The Institute closed within the year. Its files were sealed, its funding vanished, and its name was struck from the records. One moonless evening Daniel walked out the front doors and kept walking.

But a thing like that does not stay hidden. A man who never aged. A pale stranger seen only after dark, who fled from the sunrise. Blood bags missing from hospital storerooms. The stories traveled the way stories do, growing teeth in every telling, until no one remembered a lab, or a serum, or two hundred and six names. Only the shape of it remained: someone too strong, too quick, too perfect to be human, who shunned the sun and lived on blood.

People needed a word for him. They reached back into old folklore and found one.

Vampire.
